## 1. Futures Wheel

**Who proposed it:** Jerome C. Glenn, in 1972, at the Institute for the Future (IFF).

**How it works:** one starts with a central change or event (the "root disruption"). From it, first-order effects are derived — direct consequences. Each first-order effect generates second-order effects, and so on. The wheel visualizes the cascade of consequences.

**How to decide where to stop:** the method has no automatic stopping rule. Glenn suggests stopping when the effect becomes so indirect that it is no longer possible to trace a specific causal mechanism. In practice, this means the third order is the useful limit for planning; beyond that, cumulative uncertainty makes prediction useless.

**What it does not do:** it does not assign probabilities. The wheel shows possible paths, but does not say which are more likely. Without a filtering or weighting step, it produces huge and equally plausible trees, which is useless for decision-making.

## 2. Gartner Hype Cycle

**What the curve claims:** every emerging technology goes through five phases — Technology Trigger, Peak of Inflated Expectations, Trough of Disillusionment, Slope of Enlightenment, Plateau of Productivity. Gartner positions technologies along this curve annually.

**The criticism:** the curve is not empirically validated. There is no longitudinal study demonstrating that technologies in fact follow this pattern in a predictable way. Innovation economist Jack Stilgoe points out that the Hype Cycle is more of a Gartner marketing tool than a scientific model — it sells reports and positions the consultancy as an oracle. Moreover, the curve assumes every technology reaches the plateau, which is false: many die in the trough.

**What it is still useful for:** as a communication heuristic. It is useful for warning managers that initial enthusiasm is not a guarantee of delivery. It is an antidote to FOMO, not a predictive model.

**What it does not do:** it cannot be used to predict *when* a technology will materialize, nor to decide investments based solely on its position on the curve.

## 3. Gartner Magic Quadrant

**What it measures:** ability to execute and completeness of vision of technology vendors in a specific market. It places companies in four quadrants: Leaders, Visionaries, Niche Players, Challengers.

**Why it speaks of market and not future:** the Magic Quadrant is a competitive snapshot of the present. It measures who is selling well *now* and who has a convincing roadmap — but a roadmap is not a future map. A company can be a quadrant leader and be obsolete in five years if disruption comes from outside the measured market. The quadrant only sees direct competitors.

**What it does not do:** never use the Magic Quadrant to predict ruptures. It is blind to technologies that do not yet have a positioned vendor — exactly where disruption lives.

## 4. Emerging × Disruptive

**The distinction I adopted:**

| | Emerging | Disruptive |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | New, has not scaled yet, but is viable | Breaks an established value chain or logic of use |
| Can it be mature? | No | Yes, rarely |
| Practical test | "Does a working prototype already exist?" | "If this works, who loses money or power?" |

**Mature rejection criterion:** if the technology is already a commodity or market standard for more than 3 years, or if more than 50% of the target audience already uses it without surprise, it is mature. Mature technology does not enter as a root disruption — it enters as context in section 3.

A common mistake: confusing "not yet mainstream" with "emerging." 5G, for example, is not emerging — it is mature in many markets. Non-invasive high-resolution BCI is emerging.

**What this distinction does not do:** it does not say *when* the disruption will happen. A technology can be disruptive in potential and never materialize.

## 5. 1st, 2nd, and 3rd Order Effects

**What separates one order from the next:**

- **1st order:** direct, almost mechanical consequence, of the type "if X happens, Y happens next." Example: "BCI sensor becomes cheap" → "consumer devices get neural interface."
- **2nd order:** consequence of the consequence. Changes behavior, market, or institution. Example: "devices have neural interface" → "physical control industry (gamepads, mouse) shrinks."
- **3rd order:** field restructuring. Changes training, values, or social organization. Example: "control industry shrinks" → "interaction design schools stop teaching hardware ergonomics and start teaching neuroergonomics."

**What is not 3rd order:** "changes society" or "transforms everything." That is empty. A third-order effect needs to name a specific mechanism.

**What the order separation does not do:** it does not assign degree of importance. A 1st-order effect can be more impactful than a 3rd-order one. Order measures causal distance, not magnitude.

## 6. Extra Method: Delphi

**What it is:** a structured consensus-based forecasting method by experts. A group of experts answers iterative questionnaires, with anonymous feedback from the group's responses. After rounds, estimates or scenarios converge.

**Why it was not on the list:** the activity asked for at least one method found independently. Delphi is classic, created by the RAND Corporation in the 1950s for military and technological forecasts.

**What it is useful for:** when there is insufficient historical data for quantitative modeling, but distributed expertise exists. It is useful for themes with high uncertainty and low evidence base.

**What it does not do:** Delphi is not an oracle. It converges on expert consensus — and experts err, especially about rupture technologies (Tetlock showed that experts are barely better than chimps at long-term predictions). Moreover, the method is slow and expensive. It is not scalable for rapid analysis.

---

## Summary of Learning

No single method produces a reliable future map. The Futures Wheel generates the consequence tree, but does not weigh probabilities. The Hype Cycle gives a notion of maturity, but is not scientific. The Magic Quadrant measures current market, not future. The emerging/disruptive distinction prevents confusing improvement with rupture. And Delphi brings in experts, but experts also err.

The competence lies in combining the methods, knowing the limits of each, and having an adversarial step — someone or something that tries to tear down the map before it is presented as truth.
